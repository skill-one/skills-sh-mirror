# Work Sample Design

Any numeric rubric you produce here is this skill's own convention - label it as such to the hiring team, never imply a published standard. The one documented exercise pair below comes from an agency's published guidance; its point values do not.

## Choosing the format

Three formats compete for the same slot in the loop. Ranked by signal returned per hour you spend, best ratio first:

- efficiency: live mock exercise > paid take-home + debrief > unpaid take-home + debrief
- candidate drop-off, worst first: unpaid take-home > paid take-home > live mock
- your effort to build it once: live mock == take-home
- your effort per candidate: live mock (two interviewers for an hour, scored on the spot) > paid take-home (an hour grading, a debrief hour, plus raising a payment) > unpaid take-home (an hour grading, a debrief hour)
- compliance cost, highest first: unpaid take-home > paid take-home > live mock

Ties: the two build efforts are genuinely equal - each needs the scenario _and_ its scorecard written before any candidate sees it, and neither can be shortened without breaking the rubric-first rule.

Compliance cost is real here and belongs in the choice, not in a footnote:

- An unpaid exercise producing output you could actually use is unpaid labour in several jurisdictions, and an unpaid product-specific task reads as free spec work; paying converts it into a contracted engagement with a record.
- A take-home also ships you a candidate file you then store, retain, and must be able to delete on request, where a live exercise leaves only your own scorecard.

Neither is a fine to budget for - both are a review to run and a decision you cannot cheaply reverse once candidates have submitted.

| Format                             | Buys you                                                                                                    | Costs you                                                                                  | Source                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Live mock exercise (60-90 min)     | Reasoning under questioning directly - the thing the debrief exists to extract, with no artifact in between | Two interviewers live per candidate, and a house format you build and maintain             | RevPartners' documented pair, below                                          |
| Paid take-home (2-3h, around $500) | The artifact plus the debrief, with the drop-off removed                                                    | A per-candidate budget line and an approval most first reqs do not have                    | Teams paying report strong completion and no declines                        |
| Unpaid take-home (~2h)             | The artifact plus the debrief, at no budget                                                                 | Your best candidates - those with a demanding job or caring responsibilities decline first | Volm: "something that'll take 2ish hours"; three hours is the unpaid ceiling |

Deleted, not demoted: the eight-hour unpaid exercise. It "select[s] for eight uncommitted hours", and no rubric recovers the candidates it filtered out before you ever saw them. Where local rules or your own policy prohibit unpaid candidate work, that same rule deletes both take-home rows - run the live mock, and say the rows were removed rather than deprioritized.

- **Default**: the two-hour unpaid take-home plus a scored debrief.
- **Move up to paid** when candidates start declining, when the finalist pool is senior and employed, or when the prompt touches anything resembling your real backlog.
- **Move to the live mock** when you will run this req shape repeatedly and the house format amortizes across reqs.

**What this order starves**: the paid take-home - the best drop-off-to-signal trade on the page, and the row that loses every efficiency round because it needs a budget line nobody pre-approved. Promote it anyway when the finalist pool is three or fewer: the spend is bounded, and the failure it prevents - your first choice declining the exercise - is the expensive one.

**Re-rank against what you already have:**

- An existing scored live-interview format promotes the live mock to first outright.
- A team that has never run a structured debrief should not start there, since scoring and facilitation happen at once.

Whatever the ranking says, three hours is the ceiling for an unpaid exercise, "because almost all of the signal is there by hour two" - and test the cap on your own team first, because authors know the answer and candidates take roughly twice as long.

Write the rubric before the first submission arrives. The assignment "works best to set the scene, not as a take-home exam" - the debrief conversation carries the signal, not the artifact.

## Prompt design

A good prompt looks like a real defect, not a project. Give a small, messy, synthetic artifact and ask for a diagnosis, a prioritized fix list, and what the candidate would ask stakeholders before touching anything. Grade the reasoning and the stakeholder step, not the polish - the loop's red/green anchors apply here too: the weak submission jumps straight to the system change, the strong one talks to users first.

Three artifacts to fabricate, ranked by signal per hour spent building them, best ratio first:

- efficiency: messy CRM export > broken funnel report > forecast reconciliation
- your effort to build: forecast reconciliation > broken funnel report > messy CRM export (a day making three views disagree coherently > a day of wrong-but-consistent math > an hour of dirty rows)
- competency reached: forecast reconciliation == broken funnel report > messy CRM export

Ties: the two report artifacts are equal on reach because each lands exactly one competency the export cannot - forecast judgment and funnel-definition judgment - and neither reaches past its own.

- A messy CRM export: duplicate accounts, blank close dates, inconsistent stage names, orphaned contacts.
- A broken funnel report whose stage-conversion math does not reconcile.
- A forecast-reconciliation task where rep commit, manager roll-up, and finance disagree.

Pick by the competency your stage map still leaves unowned first, and by this order only when two artifacts would cover it equally. What the order starves: the forecast-reconciliation task, the only prompt probing forecast judgment on an artifact rather than in conversation - promote it when the req's top-ranked outcome is forecast accuracy.

Real tasks lifted from live postings, usable as seeds:

- A CRM opportunity-and-account field and layout clean-up (inventory fields, flag redundant/unused/inconsistently named ones, propose a revised layout plus a change-management plan).
- An opportunity risk-and-recovery scoring build.
- A data-analysis case with three to five business questions delivered as a five-to-ten-slide deck to a panel.

That last one costs the whole panel an hour on top of the candidate's preparation, so budget it as a live stage, not as a take-home.

## The one documented exercise-and-rubric pair

RevPartners, "RevOps Experts: Hiring a Revenue Operations Jedi" (Matt Bolian, Oct 2021, updated Jan 2026):

- _Mock mapping_, live: the candidate maps a fictional client's sales or marketing ops flows on a shared canvas in real time to find bottlenecks. Scored on Process (uses the house format, sets up beforehand, maps while talking, uses decision boxes, delays decisions) and Knowledge (sales ops, holding stages, marketing ops, search issues, email database). Roughly 60% pass rate.
- _Mock weekly sync_, live: a 25-minute timed meeting plus five minutes of feedback. Scored on Required items (bottom line up front; drives decisions) plus bonus items (clarity, recaps, forward momentum, tasks with what/who/when). Roughly 80% pass rate.
- Mechanics worth copying: every mock has a scorecard; two graders score independently and average; "not looking for perfect - looking for about a required + threshold".

## Grading rubric

Grade against the same anchored competency scale as the interviews, never on a separate ad-hoc impression. Dimensions one practitioner names, without attaching point values to them:

- Translation of business goals into requirements.
- Analytical rigor.
- Systems thinking.
- Clean execution.
- Stakeholder management.
- Tool fluency.

A workable convention: score each dimension on the loop's anchor scale, require cited evidence from the submission or debrief per score, and set the bar at "all required dimensions at meets-bar" rather than a summed total - a sum lets polish subsidize a missing stakeholder step.

## Debrief

Run a structured debrief: what to look for, how to score it, which follow-ups to ask. Probe the reasoning behind two or three choices in the submission ("why this fix first?", "what would you have asked the VP of Sales before doing this?") - reasoning under questioning separates a genuine author from a coached one. Score the debrief on the same rubric, and never let the work-sample score override the loop: it is one data point among many, and scoring is guidance, not absolute.
