# The Eight Elements - Evidence Bar

Per letter, this file gives:

- This skill's chosen definition.
- What each rung of the 0-3 ladder looks like.
- Assumption red flags.
- The questions that move the letter up the ladder.

Definitions and evidence standards come from the methodology's own publisher (meddicc.com) and named practitioner sources unless stated otherwise; the 0-3 mapping is a practitioner variant this skill adopts rather than official methodology doctrine.

A field rule worth applying to every letter: the answer must exist in writing or on a recorded call. Verbal recall from a hallway conversation does not count.

Each letter's "Move up" ask is one option in a single competing menu: what each costs and the order to attempt them is ranked once, in SKILL.md § Closing the biggest gap. Read the ordering there rather than re-deriving one letter at a time - per-letter order is what makes a rep spend a week on the expensive ask the day before a forecast call.

## M - Metrics

The quantified economic outcome the buyer expects, stated as a number they own. Families: economic, efficiency, risk.

- 3: a figure the buyer supplied in their own words, ideally validated by the Economic Buyer or their finance function - e.g. "we lose about $40K per month of delay" in the buyer's own email. A buyer-confirmed M2.
- 2: the buyer verbally agreed to a figure the rep proposed, once.
- 1: "they want to improve efficiency"; a rep-built ROI the customer never confirmed; a generic case-study stat (an M1) recited at the prospect.
- Red flags:
  - The metric exists nowhere as a written sentence with a number in it (Whyte's rule: not in the deal record as a number = red).
  - Metrics disconnected from the pain.
  - One-time discovery instead of continuous exploration.
- Move up: "What number will your CFO look at to call this a success?" / "Can you send me the figure you'd put in the business case?" The Three Whys - why anything, why you, why now.

## E - Economic Buyer

The single person with discretionary authority to spend, veto power over what others approve, and P&L responsibility, operating at the strategic level.

- 3 - "Sponsorship": you have met the EB, they confirmed the business case matters to them, and oversight is agreed.
- 2 - "Access": the EB is named and the champion has committed to an introduction not yet held.
- 1: "my contact says the CFO is on board"; a budget holder assumed to be the EB.
- Red flags:
  - The champion is unwilling or unable to make the intro - a major red flag that also questions the champion.
  - Pitching product features to the EB instead of business objectives and outcomes (a documented failure pattern).
  - Not noticing the EB has changed when circumstances shift.
- Move up: ask the champion to arrange the meeting - the ask is itself the test, and it scores two letters at once.

## D - Decision Criteria

The standards the buyer will use to compare options: technical, economic, and relationship.

- 3: criteria the buyer wrote down or confirmed, ideally shaped with the seller, with proof mapped against each.
- 2: the buyer described their criteria verbally, uncaptured.
- 1: a vendor feature list or an RFP requirement sheet treated as neutral criteria.
- Red flags:
  - Assuming the buyer has criteria at all - often they don't and decide on gut feel, which is the seller's opening to educate and shape.
  - Criteria drifting mid-cycle with nobody tracking the change.
  - Criteria covering only the technical dimension.
- Move up: "What would you need to see to be confident this is the right call - can we write those down together?"

## D - Decision Process

The sequence of who decides what, in what order, by when: technical validation and business approval.

- 3: a mutual action plan the buyer co-edited and agreed, with dated owners on both sides.
- 2: the buyer described the steps verbally.
- 1: a close date the rep invented; "they said Q3".
- Red flags:
  - Activity mistaken for progress - extra demos, reference calls, and reports are not advancement without buyer confirmation of movement through their process.
  - The publisher calls this letter "the number one thing that dictates your time to close".
- Move up: propose the plan and ask the buyer to edit it - the edit is the evidence.

## P - Paper Process

Everything between the verbal yes and the signature: legal, procurement, security review, contracting - process, people, timing.

- 3: a mapped path with named owners, redline and security-review timelines, and vendor-onboarding steps known.
- 2: the buyer stated the path exists and sketched it, unmapped.
- 1: "procurement is just a formality".
- Red flags:
  - MSA, security, or legal steps discovered after the yes - stalls at this stage sit inside the broader pattern Forrester documents (86% of B2B purchases stall at some point during the buying process) and the roughly 44-46% of "Commit"-forecast deals CSO Insights finds never close; no analyst firm isolates the post-verbal-yes stage specifically, so treat the share as directional.
  - Momentum lost because the rep stopped driving once the decision was "won".
- Move up: "Walk me from your yes to a signed contract - who touches it, and how long does each step usually take?"

## I - Implicate the Pain

A three-stage progression: identify the problem, indicate its broader organizational impact, implicate the buyer in the costed consequence. Unimplicated pain produces no urgency and the project gets deprioritized behind more painful ones.

- 3: the buyer states the specific, costed consequence of not solving - "we missed forecast by 15% last quarter because of this."
- 2: the buyer names the problem; the cost is unquantified.
- 1: a surface pain with no cost attached, or a pain the rep asserts on the buyer's behalf.
- Red flags:
  - No urgency anywhere in the deal (the de facto tell that pain was identified but never implicated).
  - Pain quantified only in the rep's numbers.
- Move up: past-specific cost questions ("when did that last happen, and what did it cost?"). Building the full question set is mbfinotti/sales-skills@sales-discovery-questions territory.

## C - Champion

Power and influence, a vested personal interest, and selling internally when you are not in the room. Role ladder: Contact → Coach → Champion; a coach informs and guides but lacks power - one of the most common and costly confusions in a deal.

- 3: the contact has acted - set up the EB meeting, shared internal evaluation criteria, or presented to the buying committee.
- 2: the contact says they will advocate; no observable act yet.
- 1: friendly and helpful, but has not acted and may not be able to - a coach.
- Red flags:
  - Hesitation on introductions.
  - No articulable personal win (the "skin in the game" test).
  - Over-familiarity that leaves the rep afraid to call them to task.
  - Every piece of deal evidence flowing through this one person (single-threading).
- Move up: test with an ask, because a champion is proven by action - "found" is not a score. Deeper stakeholder work belongs to mbfinotti/sales-skills@deal-champion-mapping.

## C - Competition

Any person, vendor, or initiative competing for the same funds or resources: rivals, self-build, other internal initiatives, inertia.

- 3: the buyer has articulated the alternatives; a win plan positions against each, including do-nothing, via criteria shaped with the buyer.
- 2: alternatives named by the buyer; no positioning or win plan.
- 1: "we're the only one they're looking at".
- Red flags:
  - Inertia unexamined - the do-nothing option is the most common deal-killer, and the publisher's own figure is that 60% of deals are lost to inertia (the publisher's own claim).
  - Never discussing competition with the champion.
  - Knocking competitors ("Do Not Knock Your Competition. Seriously, don't.").
- Move up: "Who benefits from keeping things exactly as they are?" / "Has building this internally come up?"

## Cross-letter checks

Run after scoring all letters, before banding:

- Single-threading: if one contact is the source of evidence for most letters, note it on the scorecard - a departing champion or a reorg kills single-threaded deals; successful deals show many engaged contacts, lost deals few (a benchmark finding).
- Compelling-event thread: is there a dated, buyer-owned reason to act visible across Pain, Metrics, and Decision Process? Its honest absence is deal intelligence to record - never manufacture one.
- Consistency: a green Decision Process with a red Economic Buyer, or green Metrics with red Pain, is usually an inflation artifact - re-challenge the green.
