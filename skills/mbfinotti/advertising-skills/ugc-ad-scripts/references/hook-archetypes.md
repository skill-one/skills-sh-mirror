# Hook Archetypes

Menu of opening moves for UGC-style ad hooks. Pick 3-5 distinct archetypes per concept - near-identical openings mostly re-test what is already known.

## The triad

Every hook is three channels working at once, not one line:

| Channel        | Job                                                       |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| Spoken line    | First words out of the performer's mouth - opens the loop |
| On-screen text | Anchors the claim for sound-off viewers                   |
| Opening visual | What is literally on screen - stops the scroll            |

- Make the three carry different information. Never caption the visual with the spoken line - a duplicated channel is a wasted channel.
- Treat the channels as interchangeable entry points, not a hierarchy; any one of them can lead. Deliberately unranked: all three cost the same to write and ship in the same take, so there is no effort to trade between them.
- The strongest hooks fire two channels at once - a direct-address spoken line over a pattern-interrupt visual attacks attention twice.
- Keep on-screen text to roughly 5-10 words per second of screen time.
- Pull the wording from evidence: a customer's own phrasing beats marketing paraphrase every time.
- Talent to camera generally outperforms voiceover over B-roll, and a screen recording of the actual product lifts performance (Savannah Sanchez, The Social Savannah).

## Archetype menu

This list decides what gets filmed, so the rows run in efficiency order - persuasion bought per unit of production effort, not cheapest first. Effort here is what must exist before the camera rolls, never the writing:

- efficiency: skeptic's turn == problem callout == POV > question == first-person confession == curiosity gap == reverse psychology == ironic list > contrarian > proof-first == silent review > bold claim > before/after contrast
- cost: before/after contrast > proof-first > silent review == bold claim > every dialogue-only archetype (near-zero - one line, no asset)
- value: proof-first == before/after contrast (they show the receipt) > skeptic's turn == problem callout == POV (they name what only this customer feels) > the framing-only rest
- compliance cost: before/after contrast (typicality substantiation, and restricted outright in health and weight-loss) > proof-first == bold claim (a documented result plus the customer's sign-off to show it) > every other archetype (baseline disclosure only)

Why the ties hold, tier by tier:

- **Skeptic's turn == problem callout == POV.** All three are one line of dialogue aimed at something only this customer feels - an objection, a struggle, a situation - so each buys recognition at identical cost. Nothing separates them but which of the three the evidence document actually gives you wording for.
- **Question == first-person confession == curiosity gap == reverse psychology == ironic list.** Same one-line cost as tier 1, and all five trade on framing rather than on anything the viewer can check, so they buy the same weaker thing. Pick by fit to the evidence, not by rank.
- **Proof-first == silent review** on efficiency only: proof-first's higher value is exactly offset by needing a cleared result rather than an hour of footage. They are not equal on any single axis - read cost and value separately before choosing.
- **Silent review == bold claim** on cost: an hour of product footage and an hour of digging out a defensible fact land in the same order of magnitude.
- **Proof-first == bold claim** on compliance: both assert a specific result, so both need it documented and signed off before it can be spoken on camera.
- **Proof-first == before/after contrast** on value: both put a receipt on screen, and a viewer weighs a shown result the same whether it arrives as one number or as two states.

**What this order starves: before/after contrast, and proof-first behind it.**

- They are the top of the value axis and the bottom of the efficiency one - the only two archetypes that show the viewer something checkable, and the two that cannot be written into existence.
- A slate filled purely by ratio is all dialogue, all framing, and never once shows a result.
- Promote them when the asset already exists and is cleared (proof-first's cost collapses to near-zero while its value is unchanged, so it leads outright), when the interview answer was a compounding mandate (the cleared result and the footage outlive the ad that used them), or after two rounds where tier 1 hooks have all landed within noise of each other - that is the market saying it has heard the framing and wants the receipt.

Ruled out means deleted, not last. Name the deletion in the script package so the next round re-tests the constraint instead of re-proposing the archetype:

- Regulated category barring the comparison - health and weight-loss most commonly - **deletes before/after contrast**.
- No product footage and no screen recording, with no way to capture either → **silent review is deleted**.
- No documented result the account is cleared to show → **proof-first and bold claim are both deleted**.

The surviving ordering is still a default, not a law: it shifts with context and with who films. Re-rank it against what you know about the user - a founder comfortable on camera, footage already shot, a cleared customer outcome - and against their answers to the deadline, durability and effort-ceiling questions in the skill's interview.

Default rung: fill three of the 3-5 slots from tier 1, and spend a remaining slot on a surviving asset-gated archetype whenever its asset already exists.

Example lines are illustrative - replace every noun and number with language sourced from the user's evidence.

**Tier 1 - dialogue-only, best ratio on the list. Start here.**

1. **Skeptic's turn** - open with doubt, pivot to the product. "I genuinely thought this was another dashboard nobody opens." One line of dialogue against the viewer's own objection pre-handled, which is what puts it top.
2. **Problem callout** - name the exact felt struggle. Spoken: "If you rewrite the same status update three times a week, stop." Watch out: generic pain is invisible; specificity is the whole mechanic.
3. **POV / relatability** - mirror a hyper-specific situation. "POV: it's 4:55 pm and the export is still running." Watch out: generic POV is invisible.

**Tier 2 - dialogue-only too, but each trades on framing rather than on something the viewer can check.**

4. **Question** - ask the question the buyer actually types into search. Watch out: use their verbatim phrasing, not a marketer's rewrite.
5. **First-person confession** - "I was doing [common thing] completely wrong." Watch out: reads fake without lived-in detail.
6. **Curiosity gap** - withhold the noun. "Nobody tells you what actually causes this." Watch out: must pay off in the body or it is clickbait that poisons conversion.
7. **Reverse psychology** - "Don't buy this if you [trait]." Qualifies the viewer and inverts the expected ad posture.
8. **Ironic list** - "3 reasons why you should NOT try this." The list then sells; the frame disarms.
9. **Contrarian** - "Everyone says X. That advice is why Y keeps happening." Costs one line like the rest of this tier, but sits last in it on variance alone: the payoff has to defend the claim or the whole script reads as a stunt.

**Tier 3 - asset-gated. Strongest proof on the list, worth a slot only once the asset exists.**

10. **Proof-first** - lead with the receipt: a result screenshot, a number, the demo money-shot. Needs a real result the account owns and is cleared to show; without one, budget a quarter of measurement work before this archetype is available at all. Strongest when the proof brags by itself.
11. **Silent review** - no spoken line; the product does something on camera while text carries the claim. Needs an hour of product footage or a screen recording. Strong on muted feeds.
12. **Bold claim** - specific and falsifiable. "This replaced my entire reporting day." Needs a fact the advertiser can defend, which can mean a week of digging or a legal read, and it must be substantiated again in the body.
13. **Before/after contrast** - two states in the first beat. The most expensive row here:
    - Two setups.
    - A before state that may no longer exist.
    - A typicality line to clear.
    - A category ban to check first.

    See the compliance checklist for the typicality rules.

## Quality bar per hook

- **Generic test: if a direct competitor could say the same opening line word-for-word, it is too generic to be worth testing.** Rewrite until it is only sayable about this product or this customer.
- Run the Four U's (general direct-response test): Unique, Urgent, Ultra-specific, Useful - hit at least two hard.
- Spoken line lands in under ~2 seconds read aloud.
- On-screen text: 3-7 words, sharpens rather than repeats the spoken line.
- Works muted: visual + text alone still communicate the premise.
- Concrete noun or specific number beats any adjective.
- Cliffhangers generally beat opening on the benefit - withholding outperforms announcing.
- Promise only what the body actually delivers.

## Opening-frame anti-patterns

- **A static frame or a logo card as frame one is the most-cited cause of a low hook rate.** Open on motion, a face, or the result.
- A slow build-up to the product wastes the only guaranteed seconds of attention.
- Footage that contradicts the line - a calm claim over frantic B-roll - reads as inauthentic even when the words are right.

## B2B skew

- Lead archetypes, in efficiency order for a professional feed: problem callout > question > proof-first > contrarian - all framed as insight or teaching, never as a pitch.
  - Problem callout and question: stay dialogue-only.
  - Proof-first: needs a customer result cleared for public use, which usually means someone else's approval.
  - Contrarian: keeps its high variance here too.
  - Re-rank when the user already has a cleared case study - proof-first then leads, since credibility is the scarce thing on these feeds.
- Credential the speaker inside the hook ("I run payroll for 40-person startups") - expertise is the B2B scroll-stopper.
- Avoid discount-urgency and haul-style openings; professional feeds reward "here's what I learned" and punish "commercial".
